Intern Handbook

A guide to your internship at Thomas Philip. You're here to observe, learn, and support — not to run legal work independently. This handbook explains what's expected of you and how to make the most of your time here. Every section ends with a short self-test.

Ground rules for interns

  • You're here to learn, not to prove yourself. No one expects you to know how to draft an affidavit on day one.
  • Watch first, then ask. Observe how the lawyers work before asking questions.
  • Confidentiality is absolute. Nothing you see or hear leaves the office. Not to family. Not to friends. Not on social media.
  • Show up on time, every day. The single simplest way to make a good impression.
Section 1

What to expect

Interns at Thomas Philip are here for a defined observation-and-support period — typically shorter and more structured than a paralegalship. Your role is to learn how a litigation practice actually works, not to produce independent legal work.

What you will do

  • Observe lawyers in court and in client meetings (when appropriate)
  • Sit in on internal case discussions and strategy sessions
  • Assist paralegals with basic administrative tasks — filing, photocopying, bundle assembly
  • Take notes during observations and share them with your supervising lawyer
  • Ask questions and read materials suggested by lawyers

What you won't (yet) do

  • Draft affidavits, submissions, or cause papers
  • Conduct independent legal research for filing
  • Translate documents that will go to court
  • Speak with clients on the firm's behalf
  • Send correspondence in the firm's name

If any of these tasks are offered to you, treat them as an opportunity to shadow the paralegal or lawyer doing them — not as tasks you complete alone.

RuleIf in doubt about whether a task is within your scope, ask before starting. It is always better to check than to overstep.
Section 2

Office etiquette

How you present and conduct yourself matters. Interns are noticed; the small things are how you build a reputation.

Dress code

Formal business attire, every day. No exceptions unless a lawyer tells you otherwise.

  • Men: long-sleeve shirt, tie, formal trousers, formal shoes.
  • Women: formal blouse/shirt, formal trousers or skirt (knee length or longer), closed formal shoes.
  • Nothing loud, distracting, or overly casual.

Hours

8.30 AM – 5.30 PM, in-office. Arrive 5–10 minutes early. Do not leave before 5.30 unless a lawyer tells you to.

Some days will run late — hearings, filings, urgent matters. This is normal. Stay unless told otherwise.

Phones

Keep your phone on silent. Personal use should be minimal during work hours — ideally only on breaks or if genuinely urgent. Never scroll social media at your desk.

Communication style

  • Address lawyers professionally — use their preferred name/title. Ask if unsure.
  • Speak clearly. Do not mumble.
  • If you don't understand something someone said, ask them to repeat — do not pretend to understand.
  • Emails and messages inside the firm should still be formal. No "hey", no emojis.

Breaks and moving around

  • Lunch is a genuine break. Take it, but stick to a reasonable time (typically 1 hour).
  • If you step out for anything else during work hours, let your supervising lawyer or a colleague know.

Q2.A lawyer says something in a meeting you don't understand. What do you do?

Section 3

Taking notes & observing

Most of your value as an intern comes from paying close attention. What you observe — how lawyers frame arguments, handle judges, respond to opposing counsel — is worth more than any textbook.

In court

  • Bring a notebook and pen. Not just your phone.
  • Sit at the back of the courtroom unless directed otherwise.
  • Note the case name, court, date, judge, and lawyers appearing.
  • Write down: what each side argued, how the judge responded, what was decided or what happens next.
  • Do not use your phone in court. Ever.
  • Stand up when the judge enters and leaves.

In client meetings (if permitted to observe)

  • Only attend if a lawyer has explicitly invited you.
  • Introduce yourself briefly at the start ("Hi, I'm [name], I'm interning here").
  • Take notes but do not interrupt.
  • Do not offer opinions unless asked.
  • Everything said stays confidential (see Section 5).

In internal case discussions

  • Listen more than you speak.
  • Write down anything you don't understand — look it up or ask afterwards.
  • If asked for an opinion, give one honestly, briefly. It's fine to say "I don't know enough about this."

What to do with your notes

  • Review them at the end of the day.
  • Share the observation notes with your supervising lawyer if useful.
  • Keep a private log of what you learned each day — you'll be surprised how much this adds up.
Self-test

Check what stuck

3 questions.

Q1.You're observing a court hearing. Can you check a WhatsApp message on your phone?
Correct: Never in court. Judges notice. It's disrespectful to the court and reflects on the firm.
Q2.You're sitting in on a client meeting. The client asks a question and pauses. What do you do?
Correct: Wait. You're not qualified to advise clients. Take notes, listen, learn.
Q3.What's the best thing to do with the notes you take during a court observation?
Correct: Review + share if useful. Never post case content publicly — that's a confidentiality breach.
Section 4

Basic admin tasks

You'll be asked to help with straightforward administrative tasks. Do them well and cheerfully — how you handle small tasks tells a firm how you'll handle bigger ones.

Photocopying & scanning

  • Check every page after copying — no missed pages, no upside-down scans.
  • Keep the original in the exact order you received it.
  • Return originals to the person who gave them to you, not left on a desk.
  • If you jam the copier, tell someone — don't leave it for the next person.

Filing

  • Files are usually organised by matter/client name. Always double-check where a document belongs.
  • If unsure, ask before filing.
  • Do not remove anything from a physical file without noting where you took it from.

Court bundle assistance

You may be asked to help a paralegal prepare a court bundle. This means:

  • Arranging documents in the order given
  • Numbering pages consistently
  • Assembling tab dividers
  • Making copies

You are helping the paralegal — they are responsible for the final bundle. Ask them exactly what they need before starting.

Answering the phone (only if asked)

If specifically asked to cover reception or answer a call:

  • "Good morning / afternoon, Thomas Philip Advocates & Solicitors, how may I help you?"
  • Take a message: caller's name, company, phone number, reason for calling, time of call.
  • Do not give out any information about matters, lawyers' schedules, or anything else. If asked, say "I'll pass your message on and someone will get back to you."
Self-test

Check what stuck

3 questions.

Q1.You're photocopying a 40-page document. Two pages come out blank. What do you do?
Correct: Reprint them. Always check every page after copying. A copy with missing pages can cause serious problems downstream.
Q2.A caller asks whether one of the firm's lawyers is in a meeting or not. What do you say?
Correct: Take a message. Never share information about lawyers' schedules or client matters over the phone.
Q3.You're helping a paralegal prepare a court bundle. Who's responsible if pages end up out of order?
Correct: The paralegal. They own the final product. But confirming their instructions upfront reduces mistakes.
Section 5

Confidentiality

This is the one rule you cannot break. Every client trusts the firm with sensitive information. That trust is professional obligation, not preference.

Absolute ruleNothing you see, hear, or read inside the firm may be shared outside it. Not to family. Not to friends. Not to law school classmates. Not on social media. Not to anyone. Ever.

What counts as confidential

  • Client names
  • Case details
  • Documents (including drafts)
  • What was said in meetings, calls, or courtrooms
  • The firm's internal strategy or opinions on cases
  • Financial information about clients or the firm
  • Personnel matters

Common ways interns accidentally breach confidentiality

Do not
  • Take photos of documents, screens, or offices
  • Post about "the interesting case I saw today" on Instagram, LinkedIn, or anywhere
  • Discuss cases with your family "as long as no names are used"
  • Leave documents visible on your desk when stepping away
  • Log into personal email or social media on firm computers
  • Forward firm emails to your personal email
Do
  • Lock your screen when stepping away
  • Shred any printed drafts you're not keeping
  • Ask before removing any document from the office
  • Keep client names out of every conversation outside the firm
If in doubtAsk yourself: could someone connect what I'm saying to a real person, matter, or company? If yes, don't share it. Not even with people you trust.
Self-test

Check what stuck

3 questions.

Q1.You observed a fascinating court hearing today. Can you tell your law school friends about it?
Correct: No. "General terms" often let people identify a matter. The safe answer is always no.
Q2.You want to post on LinkedIn: "Great day at the office — got to see a major commercial dispute in the High Court today!" Is that OK?
Correct: No. "Major commercial dispute in the High Court today" is enough for someone to figure out the matter from public listings. Don't post about firm work.
Q3.You're leaving your desk for lunch. What should you do with your computer?
Correct: Lock the screen. Anyone who walks past shouldn't be able to see what's on it. Lock it every single time you step away.
Section 6

Asking questions & learning

The interns who get the most out of their time here are the ones who ask the right questions at the right times. It's a skill worth developing early.

Good questions

  • Specific. "What made you approach that cross-examination that way?" is better than "Was that a good cross-examination?"
  • Well-timed. Not during a hearing, not when the lawyer is on a deadline. After a hearing on the way back to the office is often perfect.
  • Show you were listening. "You mentioned the section 45 point earlier — is that always a knock-out argument?" is much better than a generic question.

Bad questions

  • Things you can Google in 10 seconds ("What does 'affidavit' mean?")
  • Questions you've already been told the answer to
  • Questions asked when the lawyer is visibly stressed or busy

How to make lawyers want to teach you

  • Do the reading. If a lawyer suggests you read something, actually read it. Come back with a question or observation.
  • Follow up. "You mentioned that case yesterday — I looked it up and had a question about paragraph 12…" This is gold.
  • Take initiative on small things. If you see a printer out of paper, refill it. If you notice a lawyer is running late, offer to help set up the meeting room.
  • Be pleasant to everyone. Not just lawyers. How you treat paralegals, admin staff, receptionists is noticed.
A note on questionsEvery lawyer would rather you ask a "silly" question than make a preventable mistake. If you're unsure, ask. The one exception: don't interrupt an active hearing or a live client call. Wait.
Self-test

Check what stuck

3 questions.

Q1.Which of these is a good question to ask a lawyer?
Correct: The specific, listening-based question. It shows you were paying attention and want to understand more. That's the kind of question lawyers enjoy answering.
Q2.A lawyer is visibly rushing to prepare for a hearing in 30 minutes. You have a question. What do you do?
Correct: Wait. Timing matters. Lawyers remember interns who read the room.
Q3.A lawyer recommends you read a case. What's the best follow-up?
Correct: Read it properly and follow up. This is the single best signal you can send about being serious.
Section 7

Making the most of it

Whether this internship leads to a paralegalship or not, it's a chance to build the foundations of a real legal career. What you take away depends entirely on what you put in.

Set a personal goal for the internship

Before your first week ends, decide what you want to leave with. Some examples:

  • "I want to have observed 5 different types of court hearings."
  • "I want to understand what a typical litigation matter looks like from filing to resolution."
  • "I want to build a relationship with at least two lawyers I can approach for advice later."
  • "I want to leave with clear feedback on my strengths and what to work on."

Keep a private learning log

At the end of every day, spend 5 minutes writing down:

  • One thing you learned
  • One thing you observed that surprised you
  • One question you have

By the end of the internship you'll have a real record of how much you've grown — and useful notes for interviews and applications later.

Ask for feedback before you leave

In the last week, ask your supervising lawyer:

  • "What do you think I did well during my time here?"
  • "What would you tell me to work on if I want to become a lawyer?"
  • "Is there anything about my professional conduct I should be aware of?"

Take the answers seriously. Write them down.

The mindset that matters

  • Show up as if you already work there. Not as a visitor. As part of the team.
  • Take small tasks as seriously as big ones. A well-photocopied bundle says as much about you as a well-drafted paragraph would.
  • Be genuinely interested in the people you meet. Every lawyer here has a story worth hearing. Ask.
  • Never waste a court visit. Even boring hearings teach you how the law actually operates in practice.
Final thoughtThe interns who leave a strong impression are not usually the ones with the best grades. They're the ones who showed up on time, worked hard on small things, asked good questions, kept confidences, and treated everyone with respect. That's it. That's the whole thing.
