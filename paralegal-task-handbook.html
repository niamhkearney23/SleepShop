1.How to use this handbook

This handbook covers the day-to-day tasks you'll be given during your paralegalship. It is not a legal textbook — it is a working guide. Refer to the relevant section before starting a task, and read the "common mistakes" at the end of each section before you submit your work.

If a task falls outside what's covered here, ask your supervising lawyer first. Never guess your way through a task that has legal or procedural consequences.

Ground rules

  • Always ask if unclear. There is no penalty for a question. There is a penalty for a mistake that could have been avoided.
  • Check everything twice. Names, dates, case numbers, party references — sloppy detail work costs the firm real time.
  • Confidentiality is not negotiable. Do not discuss client matters outside the office. Do not put client information into public tools (e.g. free online translators, ChatGPT).
  • Deadlines are commitments. If you cannot meet one, flag it early — never on the day.

2.Legal research notes

A legal research note is a written summary that answers a specific legal question for a lawyer. It is not an essay. It is a structured document a lawyer can read in 3–5 minutes to make a decision.

Before you start

Get the following from the lawyer assigning the task:

  • The exact question to research (write it down verbatim)
  • The context — what matter is this for, what's the deadline
  • The scope — how deep, e.g. "quick check" vs "full memo"
  • The format expected — short note vs formal memo
RuleIf you are given a vague question ("look into unfair dismissal"), do not start research until you've asked what specifically is being decided. A wrong question wastes hours.

Structure — the IRAC format

Every research note should follow this four-part structure. Even short notes.

Standard Research Note Template To: [Lawyer's Name]
From: [Your Name]
Date: [Date]
Matter: [Client / File Reference]
Question: [The exact question you were asked to research]

ISSUE
State the legal question in one or two sentences. What are we trying to find out?

RULE
Set out the relevant law: statute (section, subsection), case law (citation, principle), or procedure. Cite everything. Do not paraphrase without citation.

APPLICATION
Apply the rule to the facts of the matter. This is where you show your thinking. Weigh the arguments both ways.

CONCLUSION
Give the lawyer your best answer to the question. If the answer is "it depends," say what it depends on.

Sources

  • CLJ Law — primary source for Malaysian case law and statutes
  • LexisNexis — secondary source; useful for commentary and cross-jurisdictional research
  • Firm precedent database — check first whether a similar research note has already been done. Do not duplicate work.
Do not useFree online legal blogs, unofficial case summaries, or general search engines as your primary source. They may point you in the right direction, but every proposition in your note must trace back to a citable statute or reported case.

Worked example (short form)

Example

Question: Can a director of a Malaysian company be personally liable for a company debt if they signed the contract in their personal capacity?

ISSUE: Whether Mr X, a director who signed a supply contract without indicating his representative capacity, is personally liable to the supplier for the RM 45,000 unpaid balance.

RULE: Section 20 of the Companies Act 2016 recognises the separate legal personality of a company. However, an individual who contracts in their own name, without disclosing that they act on behalf of a company, may be personally liable — see Kelner v Baxter (1866) LR 2 CP 174 (applied in Malaysia in [placeholder — check local authority]).

APPLICATION: The contract in our matter is signed "Mr X" with no reference to the company. The company's name does not appear in the header or the signature block. On the facts, this points strongly toward personal capacity. However, the supplier's invoices were addressed to the company — this may support an argument that both parties understood it was a company contract.

CONCLUSION: There is a real risk Mr X will be found personally liable on the contract as drafted. Recommend defence focus on the parties' course of dealing (invoices to company, payments from company account) to establish the supplier knew he acted for the company.

Common mistakes

Do
  • Answer the question that was asked
  • Cite every proposition
  • Show both sides of a close argument
  • Keep it to 2 pages unless asked otherwise
  • Read your note out loud before submitting
Don't
  • Copy-paste headnotes without reading the case
  • Bury the conclusion at the end of a wall of text
  • Give an opinion on facts the lawyer didn't ask about
  • Use "clearly" or "obviously" — the law rarely is
  • Send a first draft without proofreading

3.Translations

Most of your translation work will be between English and Bahasa Malaysia — usually court documents, exhibits, correspondence, or witness statements. Translation errors in legal documents have consequences: cases have been lost on mistranslated single words.

Golden ruleLegal translation is not general translation. Your job is accuracy, not readability. If a source sentence is awkward, your translation should be equally awkward — do not "improve" it. The court needs to see what was said, not what should have been said.

Before you start

  1. Confirm the direction (EN → BM or BM → EN)
  2. Confirm the purpose — is this for filing, for internal reference, or for the client? Filed translations need to be sworn/certified.
  3. Check whether a certified translator is needed. If the document is going to court as an exhibit and requires certification, we cannot do it internally — a certified translator must be engaged. Ask your supervising lawyer.
  4. Read the entire document before you begin. Context changes translation.

Process

  1. First pass — literal. Translate sentence by sentence, staying close to the source structure. Do not paraphrase.
  2. Second pass — accuracy check. Read source and translation side by side. Every word, every date, every name.
  3. Third pass — reading pass. Read only the translation out loud. Where it doesn't flow, check the source again — did you miss something, or was the source itself unclear?
  4. Format. Present bilingually where possible: source on left, translation on right, or paragraph by paragraph.

What you can and cannot use

Allowed
  • Bilingual legal dictionaries (Kamus Dewan, Malaysian Legal Dictionary)
  • Firm's internal glossary of standard terms
  • Reference to previous certified translations in the firm's files
  • Asking a bilingual colleague to sense-check
Not allowed
  • Google Translate, DeepL, or any free online translator with client content
  • ChatGPT, Claude, or any public AI tool with client content
  • Machine translation "cleaned up" and passed off as your own work
  • Guessing a term you don't know — always check
Why not free AI translatorsPasting client documents into free online tools breaches confidentiality — that content is stored, indexed, and used to train models. Even redacted text can leak facts. If the firm approves specific paid, enterprise-grade tools in future, this section will be updated.

Terminology consistency

Within a single document, translate the same term the same way, every time. "Perjanjian" is always "Agreement", not sometimes "Contract" and sometimes "Agreement". Keep a running glossary as you work. Add new terms to the firm's shared glossary when you encounter them.

When to escalate

  • Any term where you're not 100% confident of the equivalent
  • Ambiguous phrasing in the source that changes meaning depending on interpretation
  • Cultural/religious/customary references without a direct equivalent
  • Anything that will be filed in court and needs certification

Common mistakes

Do
  • Translate what the source says, not what it should say
  • Keep names, addresses, and case numbers exactly as-is
  • Preserve original formatting (paragraphs, numbering)
  • Flag uncertain terms with a note to the lawyer
Don't
  • Skip words you don't know — mark them and ask
  • Change dates from DD/MM/YYYY to MM/DD/YYYY (or vice versa) without confirming
  • Translate proper nouns (person or company names)
  • Send a translation without a second-pass proofread

4.Drafting basics

You will draft affidavits, letters, and cause papers. Everything you draft will be reviewed — but the more polished your draft, the more you'll be trusted with substantive work.

Before you draft

  • Look at 2–3 recent examples of the same type of document from the firm's files
  • Confirm the parties, court, and suit number
  • Read any related documents — the pleadings, previous affidavits, correspondence

Universal rules

  • One idea per paragraph. Long paragraphs get skimmed.
  • Number every paragraph for affidavits and submissions.
  • Refer to parties consistently — "the Plaintiff" throughout, not sometimes by name.
  • Dates in full — "3 October 2026", not "3/10/26".
  • Currency in full — "RM 45,000.00", not "45k".
  • Never use contractions in legal documents. "Do not", not "don't".

Common mistakes

Do
  • Match the style of similar past documents
  • Read the whole document once end-to-end after drafting
  • Check every case citation and statute reference
Don't
  • Draft from memory — always work from a template
  • Skip the proofread because "it will be reviewed anyway"
  • Use bold, italic, or underline for emphasis in cause papers

5.Court bundles

A court bundle is the physical (or digital) file of documents a lawyer takes to a hearing. Getting a bundle wrong is a fast way to embarrass the firm in open court.

Standard preparation checklist

Court Bundle Checklist
  • All documents are in chronological order (unless the lawyer specifies otherwise)
  • Every page is numbered, top-right corner, in order (1, 2, 3… not restarted per document)
  • An index is at the front, listing each document, its date, and its page number
  • Tab dividers separate each document
  • All copies are legible — no faint or cut-off pages
  • Exhibits are marked correctly (P1, P2 for Plaintiff; D1, D2 for Defendant)
  • Multiple copies prepared: one for the judge, one for the court file, one for opposing counsel, one for our own use
  • Bundles are ready 24 hours before the hearing (not the morning of)
RuleIf a document is missing, tell the lawyer as soon as you know — never the night before. A missing exhibit found the day before is a problem. Found the morning of is a crisis.

6.Correspondence

You may be asked to draft emails or letters to clients, court staff, opposing counsel, or service providers. Everything you send in the firm's name reflects on the firm.

Rules for all correspondence

  • Get sign-off before sending. Unless the lawyer has explicitly told you otherwise, always show the draft to your supervising lawyer first.
  • Tone: formal and professional. No slang, no emojis, no casual sign-offs.
  • Short is better than long. If it can be said in three sentences, do not use six.
  • Reply-all with care. Check who is on the thread before hitting send.

Standard letter template

Standard Client Letter Dear Mr / Ms [Surname],

[Subject line — bold, one line, specific]

We refer to [the specific matter / previous correspondence / meeting].

[Body: state the purpose in one paragraph. State any facts or updates in the second. State what you need from the client or what happens next in the third.]

Should you have any questions, please do not hesitate to contact us.

Yours faithfully,

THOMAS PHILIP
Advocates & Solicitors

Common mistakes

Do
  • Read the whole email out loud before sending
  • Check the recipient's name and title
  • Attach files before writing the email body
  • Use "Yours faithfully" / "Yours sincerely" correctly
Don't
  • Send confidential information to a personal email address
  • Use "Hey" or "Hi" as an opening for external correspondence
  • End with "Thanks!" or "Cheers"
  • Assume the lawyer will catch your typos

7.Escalation & feedback

When to escalate to your supervising lawyer

  • Any task where you're unclear about the scope — before you start, not after two hours
  • Any conflicting instruction from two different lawyers
  • Anything you've drafted that involves a novel legal issue — flag it, don't hide it
  • Any error you've made — flag it immediately, not tomorrow
  • Any deadline you cannot meet — flag it early, propose a solution
RuleA mistake flagged early is a fixable problem. A mistake hidden is a professional failing. This firm can handle honest errors. It cannot handle concealment.

How to receive feedback

  • Take notes when a lawyer explains a correction — don't rely on memory
  • Ask clarifying questions then, not the next time you make the same mistake
  • Keep a personal running log of feedback and check it before starting similar tasks
  • Don't take corrections personally — the work is being improved, not you

How to ask for feedback

Don't wait to be told how you're doing. Once every two weeks, ask your supervising lawyer:

  • "Is there anything I've done recently that I should do differently?"
  • "Is there work I could be taking on that I'm not?"
  • "Where would you like to see me improve over the next two weeks?"
